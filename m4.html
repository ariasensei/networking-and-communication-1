04
Signals

What is a communication signal?

A signal is a changing electrical, light, or radio quantity used to represent and carry information.

⚡

Electrical

Voltage changes travel through copper cables, such as Ethernet twisted-pair.

💡

Light

Light pulses travel through fiber-optic cable.

📡

Radio

Electromagnetic waves carry Wi‑Fi and cellular data through air.

05
Signal Types

Analog vs. digital

Analog signal

Changes continuously and can take any value within a range.

Examples: natural voice waveform, traditional AM/FM radio, analog microphone output.

Digital signal

Uses discrete levels, commonly represented as binary 0 and 1.

Examples: computer data, Ethernet frames, USB transmission.

06
Signal Properties

How do we describe a wave?

Amplitude

The wave’s strength or height. In sound, greater amplitude is commonly perceived as louder; in networking, weak received signals may cause errors.

Frequency

Number of cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). Wi‑Fi commonly uses radio bands around 2.4, 5, and 6 GHz.

Phase

The position of a wave relative to time. Phase differences matter when signals combine, interfere, or are modulated.

Period T = 1 ÷ frequency f

08
Data Flow

Transmission direction

Simplex →

Data moves in one direction only.

Example: TV broadcast to viewers.

Half-duplex ⇄

Both sides can transmit, but not at the same time.

Example: two-way radio—press to talk.

Full-duplex ⇆

Both sides transmit simultaneously.

Example: phone call and modern switched Ethernet.

09
Timing

Serial and parallel transmission

Serial

Bits travel one after another over a channel. It is efficient over distance and is used by Ethernet, USB, and most modern links.

1 → 0 → 1 → 1

Parallel

Several bits travel at once on separate paths. It can work well over short internal distances but suffers from timing skew over longer links.

1 0 1 1 ⇉

10
Signal Problems

Why does a signal arrive imperfectly?

Attenuation

Signal becomes weaker over distance.

Example: weak Wi‑Fi far from the access point.

Distortion

Parts of a signal change differently, altering its shape.

Example: degraded pulses on a poor-quality cable.

Noise / interference

Unwanted energy mixes with the intended signal.

Example: nearby devices competing in the same radio channel.

Common solutions: shorter cable runs, correct cable category, shielding, fiber, better AP placement, channel planning, and error detection.
11
Representation

Data must be encoded into signals

Computers store bits, but the medium carries voltage, light, or radio changes. Encoding defines how bit patterns become physical signals.

Line encoding

Maps bits to signal levels or transitions on a wired link. Transitions help a receiver identify bit timing.

Modulation

Changes a carrier wave’s amplitude, frequency, or phase to represent data. Wi‑Fi uses advanced digital modulation to carry many bits efficiently.

Think of encoding as converting a message into a form the chosen road can carry.

Local Area Network

What is a LAN?

A Local Area Network connects devices within a limited area such as a home, laboratory, office, school building, or small campus.

School computer laboratory

Student PCs connect to a switch. The switch connects to a server, printer, access point, and router.

Why build a LAN?

  • Share internet access and files
  • Use common printers and servers
  • Communicate quickly within the site
  • Apply centralized security and management
14
LAN Technologies

Ethernet: the wired LAN standard

How it works

  • Devices exchange data in frames.
  • Each network interface has a MAC address.
  • A switch learns which MAC addresses are reachable on each port.
  • Modern switched Ethernet normally operates full-duplex.

Real-world use

A gaming PC, teacher workstation, IP camera, or file server uses an Ethernet cable when stability, predictable latency, and consistent speed matter.

IEEE 802.3UTP / FiberFrames

15
LAN Technologies

Wi‑Fi: the wireless LAN technology

How it works

  • Stations communicate by radio through an access point.
  • The network name is the SSID.
  • Devices share airtime and avoid collisions using CSMA/CA.
  • Security should use modern authentication and encryption.

Real-world use

Students move between rooms while phones and laptops remain connected. Convenience is high, but walls, distance, congestion, and interference affect performance.

IEEE 802.11RadioMobility

17
LAN Equipment

Know each device’s job

Switch

Connects wired LAN devices and forwards frames according to destination MAC addresses.

Access Point

Connects wireless devices to the LAN and manages radio communication.

Router

Connects different IP networks—commonly the LAN to the internet—and selects paths for packets.

NIC

The network interface that lets a device connect; it has a link-specific address.

Modem / ONT

Terminates the service provider’s access connection; it is not the same job as switching.

Server

Provides services such as files, authentication, web applications, or printing.

18
Topology

How LAN devices are arranged

Star topology

PC
🖧 Switch
Printer

Most modern Ethernet LANs use a central switch. One cable failure affects one endpoint, but switch failure affects connected devices.

Other topologies

  • Bus: one shared backbone; historically used in early Ethernet.
  • Ring: devices form a loop.
  • Mesh: multiple paths improve resilience but increase cost and complexity.
  • Hybrid: combines designs—common in real organizations.
19
Access Methods

Who gets to transmit?

CSMA/CD — classic shared Ethernet

Listen, transmit if clear, detect a collision, stop, wait randomly, and try again. It applied to old half-duplex shared Ethernet. Modern switched full-duplex Ethernet does not normally have collisions.

CSMA/CA — Wi‑Fi

Listen and try to avoid collisions before sending. Wireless devices cannot reliably detect collisions while transmitting, so coordination, waiting, and acknowledgments are used.

VLANs divide one switch infrastructure

A Virtual LAN creates separate logical broadcast domains even when devices use the same physical switches.

Why schools use VLANs

They improve organization, reduce unnecessary broadcast traffic, and help enforce security policies. Communication between VLANs requires a router or Layer 3 switch and appropriate rules.

Applied Scenario

Design choice for a small school lab

Requirements

  • 30 desktop PCs
  • 1 teacher PC and network printer
  • Student phones and laptops
  • Internet access
  • Stable online assessment

Recommended solution

  • Ethernet to desktops, teacher PC, printer, and APs
  • Gigabit-capable switches for local connections
  • Wi‑Fi APs positioned for coverage and capacity
  • Router/firewall at the network edge
  • Separate staff, student, and guest VLANs

Technology should follow the need: reliability for fixed devices, mobility for portable devices, and segmentation for control.

Summary

Key takeaways

Communication signals

  • Information travels as electrical, light, or radio signals.
  • Signals may be analog or digital.
  • Amplitude, frequency, phase, bandwidth, and delay describe behavior.
  • Attenuation, distortion, and noise reduce quality.

LAN technologies

  • Ethernet serves reliable wired connections.
  • Wi‑Fi supports mobility through shared radio airtime.
  • Switches, APs, and routers perform different jobs.
  • Topology and VLAN design affect performance and security.
